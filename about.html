What this storefront demonstrates

Personalizing from the start, for the unknown user

1. Personalizing by traffic source — Onsite Experiment

Open the ad, then click through it.

Compare the homepage you land on with the one you get by typing the address.

Why. Paid traffic is expensive and it usually lands on a page built for everyone. Someone who clicked an ad for coats has told you something, and a homepage that ignores it wastes the click.

How. An onsite campaign with one rule: the visitor arrived from a paid social link. It reads the ad from the URL and changes the front page to match — hero image, headline, call to action. The copy and the image live in the platform, not in the site, so the marketer changes them without a release. Because it reads the URL, one campaign can carry every Meta ad you run at once: each ad lands on the page built for it.

4. Free-returns reassurance before checkout — Web template

In two different sizes. Then go to the basket.

The shop says something about it.

Why. Two sizes of one item is a return already in progress. The shop can help now — or find out in a fortnight when one comes back. Offering free returns before checkout is service, and it costs less than the return.

How. A rule reads the basket and looks for one style in two sizes. A Web Template fires on it. The message is chosen by the platform, not written into the page.

6. Back-in-stock request taken in conversation — Agent One, Architect

Open a product and look at the size row. Some sizes are struck through. Click one, then open the assistant and ask to be told when it is back.

You asked once, in your own words. The assistant remembers exactly which one, and the platform tells you the moment it returns — weeks or months from now.

Why. A sold-out size or option is usually a dead end: the shopper leaves, and the shop never learns what they wanted. Here they can still choose it and ask to be told when it is back — in a sentence, to the assistant, with no form.

How. The assistant remembers exactly what was asked for — this product, this variant — and writes it to the customer's profile as a request. A journey watches stock; when the variant returns, the message goes to the people who asked for that variant, not to everyone who looked at the product. Demand the shop would otherwise never see, and a sale it can still make.

8. Loyalty tier progress from a lookup table — Web template, Lookup Tables

Open the account page, set your membership to Gold, save. Then look at the top of any page.

Every page knows it, straight away.

Why. Tier is only useful if everything downstream can see it — the site, the email, the message, the recommendation. Held on the profile rather than in one campaign, it is available everywhere at once, and the VIP flag is derived from it rather than typed by anyone.

How. The profile holds which tier you are in and how many points you have. A lookup table holds what each tier is worth — the discount, the free-shipping threshold, the points multiplier. One is about you, the other is about the programme. Change the table and every campaign that quotes it updates, rather than someone re-editing each of them.

Recognised on every device — Unified Customer Database, personas

Open the account page and sign in as the traveller persona.

A known profile with trips behind it. The same person on your laptop and on your phone.

Why. Everything from here on reads the traveller's history — status, the next trip, what they usually add. Recognition is what makes the rest possible.

How. The customer number is the identifier, so signing in on any browser resolves to the same record, and every page reads from it immediately.

Bookings on the profile — Unified Customer Database, Array of Objects

Book a fare. On the confirmation, see the booking, the points and the link across to a hotel for the same city. Then open your profile in the panel.

The booking is a record — route, dates, cabin, fare, extras — not a line in an order table. The homepage now speaks to the trip.

Why. An airline that keeps every trip as a record can act before, during and after it: the seat and the bag before departure, the disruption message on the day, the return visit afterwards. Most platforms keep only the last order.

How. Each booking is written to the profile as a structured record; the current trip's date and status are on the profile as plain fields for the site and the campaigns to read.

Flight delay message with next steps — Web template (Liquid)

Telling someone their flight is delayed is a notification; telling them what to do next is service.

Cross-channel journey orchestration

9. Browse abandonment email — Architect, email · the one worth the login

1. Open three or four products here. Do not buy anything.

2. Open the console — the pill in the bottom right — and click Copy profile ID.

3. Open the browse abandonment journey and click its email.

4. Top right of the preview it says Previewing as Anonymous. Change the user, choose Profile ID, paste yours in, and reload.

The product in that email is the one you were just looking at. Its picture, its name, its link.

Nothing in that email was written for you. The product name, the image and the link are tokens, resolved per recipient at the moment of sending. Paste a different profile id in and the whole thing changes. There is one email, and it is different for everyone who receives it.

And the site did not calculate any of it. Those values are derived by the platform from ordinary page views — last visited product, its image, its URL, its category. The shop reported that a page was viewed. Working out which one mattered, and keeping it, happened here.

Now go back to the canvas and look at the whole thing. There is an entry condition, a wait, and a check that removes anyone who has bought in the meantime — because the worst abandonment email is the one that arrives after the purchase.

And the journey itself was not built by hand. It was described in a sentence and assembled by the Journey Agent — starter, waits, checks, channels, wired and connected. That saves an afternoon, which is the obvious part. The less obvious part is that it brings the patterns with it: the exit condition, the wait before the send, the check that stops the email chasing someone who has already bought. Those are the things people forget, and forgetting them is what makes an abandonment programme annoy customers instead of recovering them.
